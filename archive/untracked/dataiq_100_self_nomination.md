## Page 7: Your Q&A

What has been the single most impactful data or AI initiative you have led in the past year? Please outline what was done, how it was delivered, and the outcomes achieved. 500 words max.

The most impactful AI initiative I have led in the past year has been building responsible-AI mechanisms for Amazon's frontier foundation-model work, spanning safety evaluation, red-teaming, risk assessment, mitigation, and launch readiness. In my role as Responsible AI lead for Amazon's AGI / Frontier AI Assets organization, I help convert broad questions about frontier-model risk into measurable evaluation programs and decision mechanisms that scientists, engineers, product leaders, and senior stakeholders can use.

The work has required both technical and organizational execution. Technically, my teams and collaborators have developed evaluation approaches for safety, fairness, privacy-sensitive behavior, misuse risk, red-teaming, and model-readiness assessment. This includes frontier-risk work such as CBRN uplift evaluation, where the challenge is to determine whether model access increases a user's ability to perform harmful tasks beyond what they could do using ordinary information access. It also includes work on rigorous agentic benchmarks, where we evaluate whether emerging AI agents can be measured in a way that is realistic, reproducible, and decision-useful rather than merely impressive on paper.

Operationally, I have focused on making these evaluations useful for model development and release decisions. Responsible AI cannot be a final compliance review after a model is built. It has to become an engineering and scientific feedback loop: define risks, measure them, diagnose failure modes, connect them to mitigation strategies, and communicate tradeoffs clearly to leaders who make launch and investment decisions. This requires strong mechanisms across science, engineering, product, legal, policy, and executive stakeholders.

The outcomes include stronger safety and evaluation practices for frontier models, clearer connections between red-team findings and mitigation work, and better decision support for model-readiness discussions. This leadership is reflected publicly in Amazon Science's article on Amazon's responsible-AI pipeline, which identifies me as senior science manager and RAI lead for Amazon's AGI organization (https://www.amazon.science/blog/building-trust-into-ai). It is also reflected in third-party safety assessments of Amazon Nova Premier, where independent black-box stress testing and red-team exercises showed strong safety performance for Nova Premier (https://www.amazon.science/blog/independent-evaluations-demonstrate-nova-premiers-safety). The initiative also extends beyond Amazon through academic community building. I led the Amazon Nova AI Challenge (https://www.amazon.science/trusted-ai-challenge/), which received 100+ university applications and supported selected academic teams working on trusted AI. The official competition structure provides $250,000 research sponsorships to selected university teams, AWS credits, mentoring, and prize opportunities; across 10 participating teams, that represents at least $2.5M in direct university research sponsorship, plus additional prizes and cloud support. This created a bridge between enterprise AI deployment and independent academic inquiry, bringing external expertise into the evaluation and safety agenda.

For DataIQ, the significance is that this initiative demonstrates how data and AI leadership is changing. The frontier is not simply building bigger models; it is building the measurement, governance, and leadership systems that allow organizations to deploy powerful AI responsibly at scale.

How are you embedding AI into your organisation, and where do you see the biggest opportunities or challenges? 500 words max.

I embed AI into the organization by treating responsible AI as an operating discipline rather than a standalone review function. In large-scale AI organizations, the key challenge is not whether teams use AI; it is whether they can evaluate, improve, and govern AI systems with enough rigor to support real-world deployment. My role is to help make that possible through evaluation frameworks, red-team mechanisms, model-readiness criteria, mitigation workflows, and cross-functional decision processes.

At Amazon, this has taken different forms across Alexa, Nova, and AGI / Frontier AI Assets. In Alexa, I founded Responsible AI science efforts focused on fairer, more private, and more trustworthy customer experiences in conversational AI, including deployed mechanisms aimed at improving experiences for underserved customers. In Nova, I led Responsible AI programs for foundation models, including safety evaluations, red-teaming, fairness, privacy-sensitive behavior, external evaluation, mitigation, and third-party assessments of Nova Premier's safety. In my current role, I work across frontier-model risks, where evaluations must adapt quickly to new model capabilities and new deployment patterns.

The biggest opportunity is to make AI evaluation more scientific, continuous, and actionable. Many organizations still evaluate AI systems through disconnected benchmarks or late-stage risk reviews. The next generation of enterprise AI leadership will require integrated measurement: evaluations that expose failure modes, connect to training and mitigation, quantify uncertainty, and inform executive decisions. This is especially important for agentic AI, where models act across tools, environments, and multi-step tasks, and where static benchmark scores may not capture operational risk.

The second opportunity is to build stronger bridges between industry and academia. Industry has access to deployed systems and operational constraints; academia brings independent inquiry, methodological depth, and public scrutiny. I have tried to connect these worlds through TrustNLP, Trustworthy Speech Processing, the SemEval Unlearning Challenge, Academic Research Award collaborations, and the Amazon Nova AI Challenge. These efforts help create shared research infrastructure for bias, privacy, robustness, unlearning, red-teaming, and safety evaluation. Relevant examples are listed on my events page (https://guptarah.github.io/events.html), ACL Anthology profile (https://aclanthology.org/people/rahul-gupta/), and Amazon Science author profile (https://www.amazon.science/author/rahul-gupta).

The biggest challenge is that AI capability is moving faster than evaluation practice. Leaders need to avoid both extremes: overclaiming safety because a model passes a narrow benchmark, or blocking useful innovation because risks are described too vaguely to manage. The right path is disciplined measurement, transparent escalation, and practical mitigation. My focus is to build teams and mechanisms that make that path real in an enterprise setting.

What moment in your career taught you most about yourself as a leader? 500 words max.

The moment that taught me most about myself as a leader was founding Responsible AI science for Alexa. At the time, responsible AI was not yet a mature industrial function with settled playbooks, especially for large-scale conversational AI systems. The problems were technically complex, socially important, and organizationally ambiguous. It was not enough to publish a fairness paper or write a set of principles. The work had to become part of how real products were evaluated, improved, and launched.

That experience taught me that my strongest leadership instinct is to build structure around ambiguity. Responsible AI problems often begin as uncomfortable questions: Which users are underserved? What does fairness mean for a speech or language system? How should privacy-preserving methods affect model quality? How do we know whether a mitigation actually helps? How should leaders compare aggregate gains against subgroup harms? These questions do not come pre-packaged as metrics or roadmaps. A leader has to turn them into research questions, evaluation designs, mechanisms, and decisions.

I also learned that technical credibility and empathy have to work together. Responsible AI can easily be perceived as a blocker if it is framed only as risk control. It becomes more powerful when it helps teams build better systems. That meant partnering closely with scientists, engineers, product leaders, and executives; listening to their constraints; and then creating methods that were rigorous enough for science and practical enough for deployment.

The experience shaped how I later led Responsible AI for Amazon Nova and now for Amazon's AGI / Frontier AI Assets organization. It gave me confidence that I could operate at the intersection of research, product, governance, and community building. It also taught me that leadership is not just having the right technical answer. It is creating the conditions under which a team can repeatedly ask better questions, measure the right things, and make decisions with integrity. In Alexa, that meant translating fairness and privacy research into deployed mechanisms that improved real customer experiences, including for underserved populations.

That lesson continues to guide me. Whether I am working on frontier-model safety, red-teaming, bias evaluation, academic challenges, or patents that translate ideas into deployable mechanisms, I try to build systems where responsible AI is not ornamental. It is part of how the organization learns.
